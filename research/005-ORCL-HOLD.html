The thesis in plain terms

Oracle is spending an enormous amount of money building AI data centers — over $40 billion [O19] of buildings and servers that are currently under construction and not yet in use. The cash goes out now. The revenue shows up later, if it shows up.

Four things are true at once:

The demand is real. Contracts that Oracle has signed but not yet delivered on — the industry calls this "RPO," which just means the dollar value of work customers have committed to pay for over the coming years — grew 46% [O07] year over year, to $664 billion [O04]. That is not a rounding error or an accounting trick. Customers are committing.

The cash burn is worse than it looks. For five straight quarters, more cash has gone out of Oracle than came in [O14]. Oracle's headline says about −$5.4 billion [O12] for the most recent quarter. But strip out one-time customer prepayments [O08] (money customers paid Oracle up front, before Oracle has done the work) and the real figure is about −$16.8 billion [O13], roughly three times worse. The prepayments are real cash, but they are not repeatable — they are one-time transfers.

They are funding it by selling new shares. Oracle issued almost 5% more shares [O17] in a single quarter (July–August 2026), against roughly 1% per year [O18] historically. Every share issued makes the share you own a smaller piece of the company. Small each time, real when it compounds.

The price is not the problem. Oracle trades at about 24 times [O23] its trailing earnings, against a median of 30 times [O24] over the last six years. Meaning: for the same dollar of profit, buyers today are paying less than the recent average. Oracle sits in the bottom quarter [O25] of its own recent trading range. It is not expensive. That argument fell off the bear case today.

So the picture is: genuine demand, a reasonable price, and a company burning cash and diluting shareholders to chase it.

The thing that decides it is the one thing you can't get. Oracle does not break out how profitable its cloud infrastructure business is on its own. So there is no way, from Oracle's public filings, to check whether $40 billion of new data centers earns a good return or a bad one. Not "the answer is unclear" — the disclosure required to compute it doesn't exist. That is what the note calls an "M15 finding."

HOLD means: the demand is real enough that selling looks wrong, and the burn, dilution, and unverifiable returns are enough that calling it a buy would mean pretending you know something you don't. Conviction 2 out of 5 because the central question is genuinely open.

What Oracle is

Three revenue engines with different formulas, blended on one P&L:

  • Cloud services and license support — combines two mechanically different streams. Cloud (OCI IaaS + SaaS Fusion/NetSuite/Cerner) is metered consumption and contracted-committed. License Support is prior-period annuity, structurally declining but slowly. Q1 FY27: $11.6B Cloud (+62% YoY, of which OCI IaaS $7.4B +121% [O28])
  • $5.5B Software (−3% YoY).
  • Hardware and services — small, low-margin, transactional. Q1 FY27: $774M + $1,414M combined.
  • Consolidated Oracle P&L cannot be honestly modelled as one business. Per M04 extended to multi-segment (per 004-TRN work): segment-level cycle-position checks required.

Now shaped as an asset-heavy business (was asset-light pre-2024). $40B of construction in progress at 5/31/2026, growing. Levered ($125B debt) and issuing preferred ($4.95B outstanding; preferred div $81M/qtr Q1 FY27 vs $0 Q1 FY26 [O32]) plus equity ($19.9B ATM in Q1 FY27 [O16]) to fund the buildout. Consolidated Q1 FY27 revenue $19,345M [O33].

What the setup is

Oracle is a spread business at hyperscale-lessor scale. Same shape as a lessor at first order: raise capital, buy long-lived depreciating assets, contract the output, earn the difference between the yield on the assets and the cost of the capital.

Contracted output: $664B remaining performance obligations (RPO) at 8/31/2026, up 46% YoY. 13% expected next 12 months; weighted-average duration ~3.3 years. Real cash commitment beyond the disclosure: $11.4B of customer prepayments received in Q1 FY27 (vs $0 Q1 FY26) — customers writing checks upfront for future services.

Capital cost: 5.61% weighted-average on the $43B of senior notes issued during FY26; ~7-8% blended cost of new capital in Q1 FY27 weighted across ATM equity, prepayments, and net debt.

Depreciating asset: $122.7B gross PP&E at 5/31/2026 (up from $59.6B year prior), depreciated at Oracle's stated 6-year lives for servers. $40B of CIP not yet on the depreciation schedule.

The spread — return on the OCI capex vs marginal cost of capital — cannot be established from public disclosures (M15). Oracle does not report OCI-specific gross margin, OCI utilisation, or per-contract margin on the $664B RPO. Both sides of the spread math require inputs that are not disclosed.

  1. Five consecutive quarters of negative FCF, sourced per quarter. Q1 FY26 −$362M / Q2 FY26 −$9,967M / Q3 FY26 −$11,484M / Q4 FY26 −$1,873M / Q1 FY27 −$5,396M. Each tied to its own 10-Q; Q2 and Q3 spot-checked against the Q1 FY27 press release reconciliation. Not a trend line — five sourced observations.

  2. Adjusted FCF worse than headline by 3×. Stripping the $11.4B of one-time customer prepayments (Q1 FY27, disclosed in the Note on "Customer Prepayments and Sales of Financing Receivables") from OCF produces underlying Q1 FY27 FCF of −$16.8B, not the reported −$5.4B. Customers are pre-funding the buildout; take that funding out and the underlying cash burn is materially worse than the headline suggests.

  3. Dilution running 5× Oracle's own disclosed historical pace. 141M shares issued via ATM in Q1 FY27 alone (directly disclosed in the 10-Q Stockholders' Equity note). That is 4.9% dilution in ONE QUARTER against Oracle's own FY26 10-K stated 1.0%/year historical net dilution rate. SBC-related activity was net negative in the quarter (2M grants offset by 4M forfeitures) — ATM is the entire dilution story.

  4. Depreciation wave on $40B of construction-in-progress. FY26 10-K Note 4: gross PP&E $122.7B (doubled year-over-year); $40B in CIP not yet on the depreciation schedule; servers at 6-year lives at the extended end of the hyperscaler range for an increasingly GPU-heavy mix. Hidden after-tax drag at Oracle's own 6-year assumption: $1.81/share/year; at 5-year: $2.89; at 4-year: $4.51.

Plus the M15 disclosure gap that blocks both the return question AND the multiple validation. Oracle does not disclose OCI-specific segment margin, OCI utilisation, or counterparty concentration inside the $664B RPO. The central return question ("does the OCI capex earn its ~7-8% marginal cost of capital?") cannot be answered from public data. Neither can the multiple be validated against the RPO trajectory (Oracle 2026 is not the business the historical distribution describes; the RPO would have to substitute for the historical anchor, and RPO validation requires the same undisclosed inputs).

Oracle's own 10-K acknowledges the class: "changes in customer demand or the ability of our key customers to meet their contractual obligations ... could require evaluation of the recoverability of related long-lived assets."

On what was removed. A fifth leg — "the multiple is expensive versus history" — was carried through most of the session at "37.8× vs 17-19× median = 2× history." When the historical distribution was sourced from 320 weekly closes 2020-2026 (median 30.24×), Oracle at 23.83× trailing landed at the 26th percentile of its own recent trading range. "Expensive" is no longer an argument.

Caveat: the sourced window is 6-year (2020-2026), coincident with the beginning of Oracle's cloud/AI re-rating. Pre-2020 Oracle traded as a low-multiple legacy software business. A full 10-year window would carry a materially lower median. "26th percentile" is 26th percentile of the re-rated era, not of Oracle's full history. That is a different sentence from "cheap versus history."

The bull case that survives

  1. Trigger #1 NOT firing. 46% RPO YoY growth vs 15% threshold.
  2. $11.4B customer prepayments in Q1 FY27 (vs $0 Q1 FY26). Real cash from counterparties with contractual commitment.
  3. OCI IaaS revenue up 121% YoY [O28]. 300,000+ GPUs delivered in Q1 [O27] (~3× Q4 FY26 pace). 850MW datacenter capacity delivered [O26].
  4. FY27 guide raised at Q4 FY26 ($90B revenue confirmed [O29]; non-GAAP EPS raised $8.05 → $8.10 subsequently [O02]).
  5. License Support annuity durable. No observable evidence of decay.

Why the rating is HOLD — UNDER REVIEW rather than BUY or SELL

Not BUY. Trigger #2 is firing (FCF-neg 5 quarters, adjusted FCF −$16.8B in Q1 FY27). Dilution running 5× Oracle's own historical pace. $40B of CIP on a 6-year depreciation schedule that will hit the P&L on a timeline management can only extend so far. Central return question is M15 — cannot be answered from public data. You do not buy a business whose returns you cannot verify while it burns cash and dilutes 5% per quarter, even at a reasonable multiple.

Not SELL. Trigger #1 not firing at all. Real cash commitment via prepayments. License Support annuity durable. RPO conversion reconciles to guide. Committing to SELL when the return question is unresolved and one leg (customer concentration) could break either way is not defensible from public data alone.

  • Reasserts the pre-registered sell triggers as written; does not soften them.
  • Names the specific disclosures that would move the rating (Item 4 in research/coverage/software-infra/ORCL-final- findings.md lists them).
  • Publishes the M15 finding. The spread question cannot be closed from public disclosures alone — this is stated openly rather than obscured with a fabricated number.

1. Strongest bear case

C1. Oracle in Q1 FY27 is a levered lessor whose asset yield is unknowable and whose funding cost is anchored above the only earnings series the analyst can compute. FY26 10-K establishes 5.61% marginal cost of new debt across the $43B FY26 tranche stack; preferred coupon 6.55% pretax and non-deductible; ATM equity issuance in Q1 FY27 (141M shares, $19.9B net) placed at a $141 weighted-average price during a window at the 26th percentile of the 320-week trailing P/E distribution — management is selling equity in the bottom quartile of its own re-rated multiple.

Against that: five consecutive quarters of negative FCF totaling -$29.1B headline and worse ex-prepayments; $40B CIP off the depreciation schedule at 6-year lives at the top of the industry range; single-customer concentration plausibly 20-25% (OpenAI reported $300B commitment against $664B total RPO). The "at least $90B" FY27 revenue guide reconciles arithmetically to disclosed RPO conversion within noise, meaning the guide is not conservative. Q2 guides 30-34% implying $20.5B; H1 = $39.8B; H2 must average $25.1B/qtr — a ~23% sequential ramp on capacity that has not yet been billed. HOLD is the ceiling this evidence supports, not the floor. SELL is defensible.

2. Backwards, unsupported, or reversed claims

C2. The thesis says "RPO $138B → $664B in 12M." $138B is 5/31/2025; $664B is 8/31/2026 — that's 15 months, not 12. The 12-month YoY comparison is $455B → $664B (Q1 FY26 to Q1 FY27, +46%) or $138B → $638B (5/31/2025 to 5/31/2026).
