Process

How every note here gets made. Explicit because it is what makes the calls reviewable. Any reader can read a note plus this page and reconstruct what was checked and what was not.

1. The screen

Universe: Russell 2000 constituents pulled from the current index membership file. Market cap band $300M–$5B. From that universe, four hard filters:

  • ROIC > 8% (LTM, reported). Return on invested capital, taken from filed fundamentals, not analyst estimates. Estimate-based ROIC introduces circular logic — the screen would end up buying analyst optimism.
  • Free cash flow > 0 (LTM). Also from reported statements. A negative FCF with a high trailing P/E is often a capex-heavy asset builder — a different thesis, out of scope for this screen.
  • Total Debt / Operating EBITDA < 3.0×. Reported figure, not consensus.
  • Currently traded, non-financial, not a REIT. Financials and REITs use different accounting conventions for leverage and returns; the same filter reads differently for them, so they are excluded from the ranking pool rather than gate-forced into it.

Post-filter, the pool is ranked by a peer-adjusted P/E residual against sector median, layered with a quality composite (ROIC + inverse leverage + FCF/Sales). No momentum tiebreaker. The screen produces a candidate list, not a recommendation list.

2. The source hierarchy

Every quantitative claim in every note has a tier attached to it. The tiers exist because a number's credibility depends on where it came from.

  • TIER 1 — SEC filings, earnings releases, transcripts, company IR materials, exchange or broker records. Fact.
  • TIER 2 — named third-party research with a dated document (Valens tearsheet, sell-side, rating agency). Cite as opinion, with attribution. Never cite as fact.
  • TIER 3 — aggregators (stockanalysis.com, Yahoo Finance). Usable only to locate a Tier 1 figure. A claim that survives only on a Tier 3 citation gets cut.
  • BANNED — any prior vault document, model summary, or note. Prior work holds methods, not facts. If a prior note contains a number, the correct move is to go read the filing that number came from.

3. The trap library

Patterns the screen produces that look like value but are not. Read this before every deep-dive.

  • Melting ice cube. High ROIC and low trailing P/E on a pharmaceutical or royalty-collection business with a defined-end-date cash stream. A trailing multiple cannot price a finite annuity. Check for composition-of-matter or method-of-use patents expiring inside five years; check for royalty streams above 20% of revenue. Fix: pull the 10-K's Item 1 patent section and the ASC 606 revenue disaggregation footnote.
  • Earnings with a known end date. Same shape as the above but for non-pharma: a customer contract, a captive concession, a legacy product. If more than half of earnings depend on something with a stated end date within the horizon, the P/E discount is the market pricing the finite duration.
  • Optically cheap on a broken denominator. P/E, EV/EBIT, and ROIC all break for banks, insurers, and REITs. Ranking financials against industrials on these metrics produces false positives — hence the sector exclusion in the screen.
  • Value-trap-post-guidance-cut. A stock down more than 40% in the last twelve months while screening cheap on trailing earnings often has forward earnings about to reset. Check the consensus estimate trajectory before spending a week on a DCF.

4. The provenance discipline

Every note ships with a companion sources file (<note>-sources.md). Each quantitative claim in the note carries an inline marker like [S14]. The sources file has one row per marker, with a tier field. A checked script (vault_provenance_check.py) verifies as a hard set-equality condition:

  • every marker in the note resolves to a row
  • every row is referenced at least once
  • no BANNED tier is present
  • no TIER 3 is present without a paired TIER 1 confirmation

The check has to exit clean before the note is publishable. Notes that ship as DRAFT status have failed the check — that failure is surfaced in the note header rather than hidden.

5. Kill criteria — written before the position exists

Every published call ships with a list of kill criteria in the frontmatter. Each is a specific observation that would flip the thesis: LPI decelerating below X, a specific customer notice, a patent challenge outcome. If a criterion is triggered while a call is open, the fact of the firing goes on the record along with whether it was obeyed. Non-obedience is the most signal-heavy record in the whole track record — a thesis-invalidating event that got ignored is the failure mode that ends careers, and it stays visible here.

Kill criteria are what separates a call from a hope. Publishing an entry price and a target without them is essentially a bet without a stop.

6. What is not measured here

No Sharpe ratio, no t-statistic, no information ratio, no significance test. With a small number of calls, those numbers are meaningless — computing them would be the exact error that made the prior systematic version of this project unreliable. Instead: honest counts of what has closed, what beat its benchmark, and what didn't. When the count of closed calls reaches double digits, some of those measures become meaningful, and this page will add them then. Not before.

7. What the reader is expected to do with this

Read a note. Cross-check the sources file against the SEC filings for the claim tiers. Judge whether the reasoning holds independently of the outcome. The point is the reasoning trail. A call that pays off for a reason the analyst identified explicitly is one type of evidence. A call that pays off for a reason the analyst did not identify is a different type — and it is recorded as such in the thesis-attribution field on close (RIGHT_WRONG_REASON, meaning luck).

Research published for demonstration purposes by an individual student analyst. No capital is deployed against these calls and no positions are held. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell any security.
